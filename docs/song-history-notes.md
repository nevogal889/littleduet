# Little Duet: song history research notes

Researched on 2026-10-16 by five research agents with sources (one batch file per 20 songs, in `content/song-stories/batch-*.json`). The app shows `writtenBy`, `year`, `origin`, the kid line, the story, a fun fact and a myth-buster on each song page. Myths are recorded as myths, never as fact.

## Songs to decide on (minstrel-show origins)

- **Turkey in the Straw**: began as 'Zip Coon' (1834), whose lyrics mocked Black Americans. The parent story says so plainly.
- **Camptown Races** and **Oh! Susanna** (Stephen Foster): written for blackface minstrel shows; the original verses use racist dialect. The catalog already uses only the safe verses.

Recommendation: replace Turkey in the Straw (the tune itself carries that history) and keep the Foster songs with the first verse and chorus only. Decision needed from Nevo before launch.

## Catalog dates and attributions to correct (none changes public-domain status)

- **bow-wow-wow**: Catalog dates the words to Halliwell (1842), but the rhyme was already printed c. 1765 in Mother Goose's Melody as 'Caesar's Song' (Opie, Oxford Dictionary of Nursery Rhymes no. 138, per Representative Poetry Online). Suggest updating origin and publicDomainBasis to c. 1765; public-domain status is unaffected.
- **jack-be-nimble**: Catalog says the rhyme was printed c. 1798 (Harley manuscript). The sources found (Opie via the Traditional Ballad Index; Wikipedia) give the earliest record as a manuscript of c. 1815 (Douce MS), first printed by Halliwell in 1844. No evidence found for a c. 1798 Harley manuscript. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **rain-rain-go-away**: Catalog credits the first record to John Aubrey (1687). An earlier version appears in James Howell's proverbs (1659); Aubrey's is a different variant, and the modern 'go away' wording dates from the 19th century (Halliwell, 1844). Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **star-light-star-bright**: Catalog says 'late 19th century'. The earliest cited printing is 1866 (William Furniss), so 'mid-to-late 19th century' is more precise. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **bingo**: Catalog cites a c. 1780 version titled 'Bob Bingo'. Sources found date the earliest reference to c. 1780 sheet music attributed to William Swords, but do not confirm the title 'Bob Bingo' (early names are 'Bingo' or 'Little Bingo'). US presence is noted from 1842; the claim of the modern form in 1880s US collections was not confirmed. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **alphabet-song**: Catalog presents Bradlee as the source; he was the publisher who copyrighted the song, and the lyricist is unknown. His edition called the tune a 'German air' and credited Louis Le Maire, though the melody is the French 'Ah! vous dirai-je, maman'. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **mulberry-bush**: Catalog cites Halliwell (1840s). An earlier record appears in Robert Chambers's Popular Rhymes of Scotland (1842), as a variant of The Merry-Ma-Tanzie (per Mental Floss; not confirmed in a second source). Halliwell's printing is 1849. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **row-row-row-your-boat**: Catalog says the familiar tune was 'published by Lyte'. The 1881 Franklin Square Song Collection was compiled by J. P. McCaskey and credits Lyte, but sources do not say whether Lyte composed or adapted the tune. Public-domain status is unaffected (Lyte died 1913).
- **rock-a-bye-baby**: Sources differ slightly on the tune's first publication: the Library of Congress says 1886 (published by Charles D. Blake), while Wikipedia says probably 1884. Blake is also sometimes credited with the tune.
- **my-little-ducklings**: The catalog dates the song to the 19th century; that fits the words, but the melody is older, found in manuscripts from c. 1750 and 1784. Attributions to Ernst Anschütz or Gustav Eskuche are not supported.
- **cuckoo-calls**: Catalog says 'Published 1835'; the poem was written in 1835 but first published in 1837 (without music) and first printed with this tune in 1842. Public-domain basis is unaffected.
- **little-boy-blue**: Sources agree on c. 1744 but not on the book: Wikipedia cites Tommy Thumb's Little Song Book, the Ballad Index (after Devlin) cites Nancy Cock's Song Book; I could not confirm it in Tommy Thumb's Pretty Song Book as the catalog states.
- **mary-had-a-little-lamb**: Catalog credits the melody to Lowell Mason. His 1830s setting is no longer used; the familiar tune is the one shared with Goodnight, Ladies / Merrily We Roll Along.
- **merrily-we-roll-along**: Catalog treats the rolling-along chorus as part of Christy's 1847 song. The 1847 print was 'Farewell, Ladies'; the chorus is first documented in the 1867 publication, after Christy's death (1862), so his authorship of that chorus is unconfirmed. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **pease-porridge-hot**: Wikipedia dates Mother Goose's Melody c. 1760, matching this entry, but the catalog's Rock-a-bye Baby entry dates the same book c. 1765 (the Library of Congress also says c. 1765). Consider using one date for the book across the catalog.
- **a-tisket-a-tasket**: The 1879 date rests on one secondary source (Studwell and Baldin, The Big Band Reader, 2000); I could not find the original 1879 printing.
- **polly-put-the-kettle-on**: Catalog cites words and tune in 'A Collection of Songs' (c. 1803); the documented 1803 print is Joseph Dale's London sheet 'Molly Put the Kettle On', and I could not confirm the collection title. Barnaby Rudge (1841) gives the words only, not the tune.
- **skip-to-my-lou**: Carl Sandburg wrote that it was a popular party game in Abraham Lincoln's Indiana youth (1810s-1820s), which would place it before the 1840s; this is anecdotal and unverified.
- **the-muffin-man**: Mental Floss reports an earlier printing of the verse, with different wording, in an 1819 book; the catalog's claim that the c. 1820 manuscript includes the tune is unverified.
- **are-you-sleeping**: Catalog says printed c.1780. The c.1780 source is a manuscript (dated c.1775-1785 by the Bibliotheque nationale, titled Frere Blaise); the first known printing is 1811 (James Fuld). Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **here-we-go-looby-loo**: Earlier than Gomme (1894): the Ballad Index cites a text in Chambers' Popular Rhymes of Scotland (1870 edition), and the Opies date the dance to at least 1745. Gomme lists the game under the title Lubin. Strengthens the public-domain basis.
- **little-bo-peep**: Consistent. Slightly earlier records exist: a c.1805 manuscript of the first verse (Wikipedia) and an 1806 printing cited by the Opies (Ballad Index), both before Gammer Gurton's Garland (1810).
- **this-old-man**: The 1906 English Folk-Songs for Schools printing is supported only by Wikipedia; the library records checked list no contents. Gilchrist learned it from a Welsh nursemaid and published her note in 1937; Cecil Sharp also collected it in Norfolk in 1911. The NOTES-content.md file cited in publicDomainBasis does not exist in the repo.
- **all-the-pretty-little-horses**: Resolves the catalog's unverified print date: earliest print found is 1903 (Maud McKnight Lindsay, A White Dove), per Wikipedia; the Ballad Index's earliest source is 1918 (Cecil Sharp). Both pre-1931, consistent with the public-domain basis. Nineteenth-century circulation is inferred, not documented.
- **did-you-ever-see-a-lassie**: Catalog says the words were printed in the US by the 1890s and calls it Scottish/American. Sources say it was first collected in the US in the 1890s, with the earliest cited print in 1909 (Bancroft); a Scottish origin is doubtful. Tune date c.1800 confirmed.
- **pop-goes-the-weasel**: Catalog says published 1852 and calls it a music-hall tune. It was a dance craze from 1852, but the earliest known printed music is 1853 (Jullien & Co.); the music-hall link comes from later verses about London venues. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **the-grand-old-duke-of-york**: Catalog calls Rackham's 1913 Mother Goose 'an early form'; it is the first printing of the modern form. The earliest related text is Old Tarlton's Song (1642), and a Duke of York version was noted in 1892.
- **go-tell-aunt-rhody**: Catalog dates the American song to the 1840s; no source found confirms that. The earliest documented Aunt Rhody texts are c.1913 (Journal of American Folklore) and 1918 (Sharp). The 1752 Rousseau melody is confirmed.
- **hot-cross-buns**: Catalog gives the Christmas Box (1798) as the print source; an earlier printing exists in the London Chronicle, June 1767. Public-domain status is unaffected.
- **lightly-row**: Catalog dates the melody to the 19th century; the earliest evidence is a manuscript dance tune dated 30 June 1784 (German Wikipedia, citing Erk and Bohme). The German words are by Franz Wiedemann (1860). The earliest English Lightly Row print confirmed is 1914; the 19th-century songbook claim was not verified.
- **moonlight-au-clair-de-la-lune**: Catalog says printed c.1780s; the earliest printing found is 1820 (Les Voitures versees). The song is generally dated to the 18th century, which still supports the public-domain basis.
- **camptown-races**: Consistent. Editorial flag: the song was written for blackface minstrel shows and its original lyrics used a racist dialect. The catalog's standard-spelling words avoid this, but the history may warrant care in parent-facing copy.
- **jolly-old-saint-nicholas**: Catalog calls the tune traditional; it is credited to James R. Murray, first published in School Chimes (1874). Published in 1874, it is public domain either way. The c.1865 date for the words matches Miller's poem (December 1865).
- **michael-row-the-boat**: Catalog says Georgia Sea Islands; it was noted on St. Helena Island in the South Carolina Sea Islands. The 1867 publication in Slave Songs of the United States is confirmed.
- **oh-dear-what-can-the-matter-be**: Catalog says printed in Edinburgh and London c.1792 via the Scots Musical Museum. Sources confirm a 1792 London printing (The British Lyre); I could not confirm the song itself in the Scots Musical Museum, only Stenhouse's later notes recording Anglo-Scottish circulation. Date and public-domain basis unaffected.
- **alouette**: Catalog labels it a French-Canadian folk song. Its origin is actually debated: it was first printed in Montreal (1879), but Barbeau believed it originated in France. Public-domain basis unaffected.
- **ode-to-joy**: Catalog public-domain basis says published 1824. 1824 is the premiere; the score was first published in 1826 (B. Schott's Sohne, Mainz). Public-domain status unaffected.
- **oh-susanna**: Consistent with catalog. Note for content team: the song was written for the minstrel stage and some original verses contain racist language; keep to the first verse and chorus as the catalog specifies.
- **sur-le-pont-davignon**: Catalog's '15th-century roots' is a claim repeated by tourism and encyclopedia sources but not firmly documented. One source (Avignon tourism office) even says Adam composed the tune; others say he took up an existing song. Treat the pre-1853 history as uncertain.
- **shoo-fly-dont-bother-me**: Catalog credits Bishop with the words and 'Frank Campbell / Billy Reeves' with the music. The 1869 White, Smith & Perry sheet credits Reeves (words), Campbell (music) and Howard (arranger); Bishop's is a separate competing claim. I could not verify that the morning-star last line is the 1869 original (Wikipedia cites it from an 1889 version) or the death dates of Reeves and Campbell. Public domain still holds on the 1869 publication.
- **up-on-the-housetop**: Catalog public-domain basis says published 1864. Hanby wrote it in 1864; the first publication found is October 1866 (Root and Cady, The Song Birds series). Public-domain status unaffected.
- **surprise-symphony**: Catalog public-domain basis says published 1791. 1791 is the composition year; the premiere was 23 March 1792, and I did not find a 1791 publication. Public-domain status unaffected.
- **the-bear-went-over-the-mountain**: Catalog dates the words to the 19th century; I found no source dating the bear words, so that remains unconfirmed. The tune facts (French, popular in the 1780s) agree with the catalog.
- **shell-be-coming-round-the-mountain**: Consistent with catalog. The spiritual source and Sandburg's railroad account rest mainly on one source (Wikipedia, citing Sandburg); the 1927 printing is confirmed separately.
- **cockles-and-mussels-molly-malone**: Catalog says 'attributed to James Yorkston, published in Cambridge, Massachusetts (1884)'. The Yorkston credit comes from the 1884 London edition (Francis Brothers and Day, arr. Edmund Forman); the 1884 Cambridge, Mass. printing (Students' Songs) is a separate variant, and the earliest known printing is Boston, 1876 (Carmina Collegensia). Public-domain status unaffected.
- **home-on-the-range**: The Kansas Historical Society dates the earliest documented printings to 1874 (Kirwin Chief, March 1874; Smith County Pioneer early that year). Other sources say 1873, so the catalog's 1873 should be read as 1873 or 1874.
- **joy-to-the-world**: Mason first paired this tune with Watts's words in Occasional Psalm and Hymn Tunes (1836); his 1839 Modern Psalmist version, cited in the catalog, is a later, very similar printing. The date of the arrangement is better given as 1836-1839.
- **my-bonnie**: The catalog calls it a 'Scottish folk song', but no Scottish source has been found. Folklorists point to an 1860s English/Irish stage song ('My Barney Lies over the Ocean') reworked by American college singers in the 1880s; best described as traditional, origin uncertain.
- **clementine**: Consistent with the catalog. Note that authorship is uncertain: the 1863 'Down by the River Liv'd a Maiden' (credited to H. S. Thompson) and an 1885 version credited to Barker Bradford also exist.
- **angels-we-have-heard-on-high**: The catalog's '18th century' is not documented: the earliest known printings are 1805 (Wikipedia) or 1842 (Hymnology Archive, Choix de cantiques, Paris). Chadwick's English text was first printed in 1860 (Holy Family Hymns) and appeared revised in 1862 (The Crown of Jesus).
- **deck-the-halls**: The catalog's '16th century' is a traditional claim without documentary support; the earliest written source is John Parry's 18th-century manuscript, and the first printing with words is Edward Jones's Musical and Poetical Relicks of the Welsh Bards (1794).
- **hark-the-herald-angels-sing**: Consistent with the catalog. Cummings made the adaptation in 1855; Hymnary.org dates it to 1856, probably the year it was first published.
- **mountain-king**: Grieg's original is in B minor; the catalog's A minor is a transposition for the app, not the original key. The play with this music premiered on 24 February 1876.
- **minuet-in-g**: The catalog's 'Written 1725' is really the date it was copied into the Anna Magdalena Bach notebook; Petzold's suite may date from around 1720.
- **polly-wolly-doodle**: Consistent with the catalog. Wikipedia claims an 1843 premiere by Emmett's Virginia Minstrels, but the Traditional Tune Archive treats Emmett's authorship as unproven and names the 1880 Harvard book (ed. William H. Hills) as perhaps the first printing.
- **silent-night**: Mohr wrote the words in 1816 (confirmed by a manuscript found in 1995), not 1818. The song was first performed in 1818 but first printed in 1833 (Four Genuine Tyrolean Songs), so 'Published 1818' should read 'first performed 1818'.
- **the-blue-danube-waltz**: Strauss composed it in 1866; 1867 is the year of the premiere and publication. Otherwise consistent.
- **turkey-in-the-straw**: Consistent with the catalog. Authorship of 'Zip Coon' is disputed (George Washington Dixon, Bob Farrell and George Nichols all claimed it), and the tune may draw on older British or Irish airs.
- **yankee-doodle**: The catalog says 1760s-1770s; the earliest verses may date to the mid-1750s (1755 or 1758 are often given), so '1750s-1770s' is safer.
