# The science of music learning at ages 2–6, and of parent–child music making

*Little Duet research. Compiled 8 Nov 2026. Every source below was opened and read. Search snippets are not cited.*

**How to read this document**
- **Strength.** *Strong*: a meta-analysis or several RCTs that agree, or an official guideline. *Moderate*: one decent RCT or experiment, or a large longitudinal cohort. *Weak*: a small, quasi-experimental or pilot study, a press-release-level source, or expert or tradition-based practice.
- **Design label.** Each finding is labelled **[causal]** (randomised or experimental manipulation) or **[correlational]** (observational, cohort or cross-sectional). Sample sizes are given wherever the source reported them.
- **Limits of what we read.** Some primary papers (PubMed/PMC pages) were blocked by bot checks. Where that happened we cite the author's lab copy, a repository copy or a university press release, and we say which.

## 1. Developmental milestones in music, ages 2–6

### Steady beat and rhythm
- **Under about age 4, most children can only keep a beat close to their own natural tempo.** Children aged 18 months to 4 years synchronised only when the beat was near their spontaneous finger-tapping tempo (about 400 ms between beats, roughly 150 bpm). Three-year-olds synchronised only in a narrow band near 500 ms (about 120 bpm). Speeding up is easier than slowing down at every age. After age 4, variability falls and the range of tempos a child can follow widens. *Moderate (a review of several small experiments).* [Provasi, Anderson & Barbu-Roth 2014, Frontiers review](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01048/full)
- **Drumming with a live human partner improves synchronisation at ages 2.5, 3.5 and 4.5, compared with a drum machine or a speaker.** Even 2.5-year-olds moved toward a tempo outside their natural range when the partner was a person. *Moderate [causal]: within-study experimental conditions, n = 36.* [Kirschner & Tomasello 2009, JECP (Duke abstract)](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458233); details in [Provasi et al. 2014](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01048/full)
- **A large minority of 3–5-year-olds cannot yet drum in time with an adult.** In one study, 13 of 35 children aged 3–4 (37%) failed to synchronise at both test tempos (2.5 Hz and 1.67 Hz). In a later sample, 37 of 156 children aged 3–5 (24%) failed at both rates (400 ms and 600 ms), and another 56 managed only one rate. Children who managed only one rate were about 0.4 years younger on average. *Moderate [correlational]: data are cross-sectional.* [Woodruff Carr et al. 2014, PNAS](https://brainvolts.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/boxtrx/PNAS-2014-Woodruff%20Carr-1406219111.pdf); [Bonacina et al. 2021, npj Science of Learning](https://brainvolts.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Bonacina_et_al-2021-npj_Science_of_Learning.pdf)
- **By about 5.5, a march-and-clap beat task comes close to adult level.** In a test of marching on every beat and clapping on every other beat at 92 and 138 bpm, children over 5.5 scored clearly higher than younger children, and the youngest were the least consistent from one test to the next. *Weak: pilot, n = 10, aged 4–6.* [Tuominen et al. 2020, Music & Science](https://ukkinstituutti.fi/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/2059204320962281.pdf)
- **Infants move rhythmically to music more than to speech, but true beat synchronisation comes much later.** Infants aged 5 months to 2 years moved more to music and rhythm than to speech. Those whose movement matched the music more closely smiled more. *Weak for us (press release; infants).* [Zentner & Eerola 2010, PNAS, via ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/03/100315161925.htm)

### Singing and pitch
- **Two- and three-year-olds sing in phrases and fragments before whole songs.** Two-year-olds sing short phrases with a narrow pitch range, a loose sense of key and a falling contour. Three-year-olds mix their own improvisation with pieces of familiar songs ("pot-pourri" or "outline" songs). Some children with a rich singing environment can imitate whole songs modelled by their mother by age 3. Phrases were sung more accurately than whole songs (n = 28, aged 2.6–3.3). *Moderate (developmental research synthesised in a review).* [Welch 2011, IOE slides](https://www.imerc.org/papers/nsp/ioe_gfw_goldsmiths2011.pdf); [Welch 2009](https://www.imerc.org/papers/nsp/welch2009.pdf)
- **Children sing more accurately when a task is split into single pitches, echoed contours or short fragments than when they match a complete song.** In a three-year UK study (n = 184), children improved little at matching whole songs but improved year on year at fragment and echo tasks. Inaccurate singing is still common at age 7 (about 30%), and boys outnumber girls about 2–3 to 1. *Moderate [correlational/descriptive].* [Welch 2009](https://www.imerc.org/papers/nsp/welch2009.pdf)
- **Pitch matching is weakest in kindergarten and better on a neutral syllable than with words.** On a 24-point scale, kindergartners averaged 10.6 on a neutral syllable and 9.5 with text, against about 18 for fourth graders. The neutral syllable was more accurate overall. *Moderate [descriptive]: n = 137, aged 4–9.* [Pereira & Rodrigues 2019, Music Perception (CORE copy)](https://core.ac.uk/download/322889247.pdf)
- **Young children's comfortable singing range is narrow and sits around D4–A4.** Rutkowski's stages run from speaking-range singers (about A3–C4) to limited-range singers (D4–F4) and initial-range singers (D4–A4); extended-range singers go above B♭4. Older work found children improved most when singing was kept between middle C and the A above it. Comfortable pitches cluster around C4–D4. *Moderate (consistent across the pedagogy literature, but the ranges are not tied to exact ages).* [Welch 2009](https://www.imerc.org/papers/nsp/welch2009.pdf); [Killian & Buckner 2008, literature review](https://usf.edu/arts/music/documents/meri-archives/962-meri-02pp11-20.pdf); [Pereira & Rodrigues 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/322889247.pdf)
- **Singing ranges grow with practice.** In the Sing Up programme, comfortable singing ranges grew by about three semitones within a few months. *Weak–moderate (school-age, large programme evaluation).* [Welch 2009](https://www.imerc.org/papers/nsp/welch2009.pdf)

### Fine motor readiness for keys (weakest area of evidence)
- **General fine-motor milestones**, each something at least 75% of children can do by that age:
  - 2 years: holds an object in one hand while using the other.
  - 2½ years: twists objects and turns book pages one at a time.
  - 3 years: draws a circle after being shown.
  - 4 years: holds a crayon between fingers and thumb (not a fist).
  - 5 years: buttons some buttons.

  *Strong as population norms, but indirect for piano.* [CDC 4-year checklist](https://cdc.gov/act-early/digital-online-checklist/4-years.html); [CDC milestones 1–5 (NJ DoE adaptation)](https://www.nj.gov/education/specialed/monitor/docs/CDCMilestones.pdf)
- **Finger independence is still immature well past age 6.** In a thumb-to-finger sequencing test, the speed gap between left and right hands was still about 3.3 s at age 7 and shrank to 0.5 s by 14 (n = 130). Physiological mirror movements, where the other hand copies involuntarily, are normal in childhood and are attributed to an immature corpus callosum. Cited work says they "dramatically decrease and stabilize by around 8.5 years." *Moderate (indirect: none of these studies tested children under 5).* [Roeder et al. 2007, Child Neuropsychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2564283); [Galléa et al. 2011, J Neurol](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00415-011-6107-9); [Nobusako et al. 2025, Front Hum Neurosci](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2025.1620941/full)
- **Gap:** we found no peer-reviewed study of piano or keyboard finger skills at ages 2–5. Age guidance for "finger numbers" comes from tradition, not data. Suzuki lessons start "by age three", but on violin and with a parent present ([UWSP Suzuki handbook](https://www3.uwsp.edu/suzuki/Documents/research/handbook/Approach.pdf)).

### Synthesis: what is realistic at each age
This is our inference from the findings above, not a published norm.

| Age | Beat / rhythm | Singing | Keys / hands |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Bounces and moves to music. Syncs only near own tempo (~120–150 bpm), better with a live partner. | Fragments and short phrases, narrow range, falling contour. | One-hand-holds-while-other-acts. Whole-hand or palm presses. A single finger is unreliable. |
| 3 | Syncs in a narrow tempo band near ~500 ms (~120 bpm). Many cannot follow an adult beat. | Pot-pourri songs. Echoes of short phrases work better than whole songs. | Pointing with one finger is plausible. Mirror movements are normal. |
| 4 | Syncing improves, but ~1 in 4 children aged 3–5 still cannot follow at either test tempo. | Says some words from songs (CDC). Range roughly D4–A4. | Crayon held in a finger-and-thumb grip (CDC). Single-finger play on large targets. |
| 5–6 | Near adult level on simple march-and-clap by ~5.5. Can slow down. | "Sings, dances, or acts for you" (CDC, 5 years). Pitch matching still often inaccurate. | Simple finger sequences are possible but asymmetric. Two-hand independence is still developing. |

## 2. Parent–child music making: effects and how much to trust them

### Infant and toddler classes
- **Active, participatory parent–infant music classes beat passive music exposure on musical, communicative and social measures.** Infants were assigned from 6 months to 6 months of either a Suzuki-based class (parents sing, move and play percussion with their infant and repeat the activities daily at home) or a passive class (background music during play). The active group:
  - showed a preference for tonal music (the passive group did not);
  - gained more in communicative gestures (parent report);
  - showed less distress and was easier to soothe (parent report).

  **Caveats:** only 34 of 49 starting families completed testing (20 active, 14 passive). Assignment was by class day, so it was quasi-random. The conditions also differed in repetition, music quality and parent journaling. Baseline gesture scores differed, the tonality interaction was only marginal (p = .09), and the social outcomes were parent-reported. *Moderate [causal, with caveats].* [Gerry, Unrau & Trainor 2012, Dev Sci (lab PDF)](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/GerryUnrauTrainor_2012.pdf); [lab abstract](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/GerryEtAl2012)
- **A year of Music Together classes increased parent–child conversational turns more than play-date classes.** n = 89 dyads, aged 9–15 months at the start, randomised. The biggest difference came in the first 6 months. Parent verbal quality improved later in the year. A follow-up at 24 months still showed a larger increase in turns per episode for the music group. **Caveats:** this was a secondary analysis. Child language itself was not measured. The sample was mostly white, college-educated and middle- to upper-income, and turns were coded from short lab play sessions. No effect sizes were in the summaries we read. *Moderate [causal] on the parent–child interaction outcome only.* [Smith et al. 2023, Child Development (SRCD Q&A)](https://www.srcd.org/news/child-development-journal-qa-music-intervention-programs-can-enhance-parent-and-baby-language); [Smith et al. 2025, Infant & Child Dev](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-impact-of-a-community-based-music-program-on-the-quality-of-p/)
- **Moving in sync with an adult makes 14-month-olds more likely to help that adult.** Infants bounced in synchrony with an experimenter handed back dropped objects more often than infants bounced out of sync (n = 48). *Moderate [causal], but lab-based and short-term.* [Cirelli, Einarson & Trainor 2014, Dev Sci](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/CirelliEtAl2014)
- **Singing kept 6–9-month-olds calm about twice as long as speech** (about 9 min against about 4 min; n = 30). These were recordings in a sparse lab. *Weak–moderate [causal] (press release).* [Corbeil, Trehub & Peretz 2015, Infancy (ScienceDaily)](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/10/151028054532.htm)

### Preschool age
- **Joint music making increased spontaneous helping and cooperation among 4-year-olds.** 96 children in 48 same-sex pairs did 3 minutes of joint singing and drumming to a steady 115 bpm beat, compared with a matched non-musical game. Afterwards the music pairs helped more (χ² = 9.92) and cooperated more (χ² = 6.04). **Caveats:** no effect sizes were reported, 15 pairs were excluded, the outcome was measured immediately after, one cultural setting was tested, and the mechanism is unknown. The partners were peers, not parents. *Moderate [causal], short-term.* [Kirschner & Tomasello 2010, Evol Hum Behav (MPI PDF)](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/Elsevier/Kirschner_Joint_EvolHumBeh_2010_1552719.pdf)
- **Shared home music at ages 2–3 is only weakly associated with outcomes at 4–5.** n = 3,031, from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children. After demographic controls and home reading, the standardised betas were: prosocial skills .10, attentional regulation .07, numeracy .05. Vocabulary and emotional regulation were not significant once reading was controlled. Book reading predicted vocabulary far better (β = .15). **Caveats:** a single parent-report item that mixes listening with music-making. The authors call the study "exploratory and correlational (rather than causal)", R² was about .04–.05, there was no baseline control, and parenting quality was not measured. *Moderate as an association; it does not show cause.* [Williams et al. 2015, ECRQ (UCL author copy)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1496686/1/Welch_final.pdf); [UCL record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1496686/)
- **Kindermusik enrolment was associated with better self-regulation and more self-regulatory private speech at ages 3–4.** n = 89 (42 enrolled now or before, 47 never). **Caveats:** families chose whether to enrol, so the groups may differ for other reasons. *Weak [correlational, quasi-experimental].* [Winsler, Ducenne & Koury 2011 (ArtsEdSearch summary)](https://www.artsedsearch.org/?p=1707)
- **Six weeks of parent-attended preschool music classes produced no reliable cognitive benefit in two randomised trials.** The outcomes were spatial reasoning, visual form, numerical discrimination and vocabulary. Trial 1's spatial advantage did not replicate, and combined, the music groups did no better than either control. *Moderate [causal, null].* [Mehr et al. 2013, PLoS ONE (DOAJ abstract)](https://doaj.org/article/06f7cedd58de4b2988c4a8d83c05cc3d)

### Context
- **Most parents already sing to their young children every day.** 69% of 945 US parents of children aged 1–31 months sang daily (72% of mothers, 59% of fathers). There was no meaningful difference by income, education or ethnicity. Parents sang daily more often to younger children. The study did not measure parents' musical training. *Moderate [descriptive].* [Yan et al. 2021, Phil Trans R Soc B](https://www.harvardlds.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/2021_YanEtAl_PTRSB.pdf)

**Bottom line for section 2.** The best causal evidence covers **social and interaction outcomes measured right after or during the activity**: helping, cooperation, conversational turns and soothing. **Long-run cognitive and language benefits are not established.** The cohort associations are real but very small, and the brief-class RCT was null.

## 3. Practices that work at this age

- **Singing and movement come before instruments, and sound comes before symbol.**
  - Kodály: the voice comes first ("the most natural instrument"), skills build in a sequence, and early training uses the pentatonic scale.
  - Orff: start with play, imitation and exploration. Rote learning is valid in its own right, and notation follows music-making.
  - Dalcroze: learning comes through movement in space, with the teacher adapting the music to what the children do.

  *Weak as efficacy evidence (these are traditions and expert consensus, not trials).* [NAfME 2024, Kodály, Orff, Dalcroze](https://nafme.org/blog/kodaly-orff-and-dalcroze-a-whos-who-and-whats-what/)
- **Suzuki: the parent attends lessons and acts as home teacher.** The method also uses daily listening, constant repetition of old pieces, small steps for "a total sense of success", group learning ("children love to do what they see other children do") and reading only once ear and playing skills are established. *Weak (method description).* [UWSP Aber Suzuki Center handbook](https://www3.uwsp.edu/suzuki/Documents/research/handbook/Approach.pdf). Independent commentary finds parental involvement benefits lessons in general, not only Suzuki, while other Suzuki tenets, such as building character or the view that aptitude is not genetic, lack support. *Weak.* [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/does-the-suzuki-method-work-for-kids-learning-an-instrument-parental-involvement-is-good-but-other-aspects-less-so-111995)
- **Repeating the same song works better than varying it.** Three-year-olds who heard the *same* story three times learned and kept new words. Children who heard three *different* stories with the same words learned none. *Moderate [causal] (small sample; abstract read).* [Horst, Parsons & Bryan 2011, Frontiers](https://doaj.org/article/0b7e5d967574458bacca6716c88db682)
- **Spacing beats massing, even at 3.** 36 three-year-olds learned novel words better when exposures were spaced 30 s apart than when massed (partial η² = .69). The retention interval was short. *Moderate [causal].* [Vlach, Sandhofer & Kornell 2008, Cognition](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Sandhofer_Kornell_2008.pdf)
- **Short daily sessions are workable.** A rhythm, singing and movement programme of **10 minutes a day** (two tasks of about 5 minutes each) for 20 weeks raised word-level phonological awareness in children aged 5–6 as much as a dedicated phonics programme (d ≈ 0.9 against 0.6; sports control). This was n = 41 with 25% attrition. *Weak–moderate [causal].* [Degé & Schwarzer 2011, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00124/full)
- **Call-and-response and echoing short fragments suit how children learn to sing.** Fragment and echo tasks produced year-on-year gains where whole-song matching did not (see section 1). *Moderate.* [Welch 2009](https://www.imerc.org/papers/nsp/welch2009.pdf)
- **A live, responsive partner is the active ingredient.**
  - Synchrony is better with a human partner than with a machine ([Kirschner & Tomasello 2009](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458233)).
  - Active classes beat passive exposure ([Gerry et al. 2012](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/GerryUnrauTrainor_2012.pdf)).
  - Toddlers aged 1–2 learned words and patterns from *live* video chat but not from prerecorded video with fake pauses (n = 60) ([Myers et al. 2016, via ISPR press release](https://ispr.info/2016/07/19/new-study-of-toddlers-sheds-light-on-value-of-facetime-video-chat-as-meaningful-interaction/)).

  *Moderate (consistent across studies).*

## 4. What makes non-musician parents effective co-teachers

- **Process praise from parents to 1–3-year-olds predicts healthier motivation years later. Person praise does not.**
  - Process praise means praising effort, strategy or action. Person praise means praising a fixed trait ("you're so smart").
  - Homes of 53 children were filmed at 14, 26 and 38 months.
  - The *proportion* of process praise predicted incremental mindsets at 7–8 (r = .34) and, through mindset, grade-4 maths and reading comprehension.
  - The total amount of praise did not matter. Person praise was unrelated.

  **Caveats:** a small sample, and the design is correlational. *Moderate [correlational].* [Gunderson et al. 2013 (UChicago release)](https://news.uchicago.edu/article/2013/02/12/parents-who-praise-effort-can-bolster-children-s-persistence-self-belief); [Gunderson et al. 2017/18, Dev Psych](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5826820)
- **Experimental support at age 4.** 4-year-olds praised generically ("You are a good drawer") coped worse with later mistakes and proposed fewer fixes than children praised for the act ("You did a good job drawing") (n = 24). *Moderate [causal], small (secondary summary).* [Cimpian et al. 2007, Psych Sci (BPS Research Digest)](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children)
- **"Doing" language beats identity language for persistence.** In four experiments with 4–6-year-olds (n ≈ 500), "let's *do* science" made girls more persistent after setbacks than "let's *be* scientists". For Little Duet this suggests "let's play the piano" rather than "you're a little pianist". Effects for boys were inconsistent, and in a caregiving domain there was none. This is our inference applied to music. *Moderate [causal] (domain-specific).* [Rhodes et al. 2019, Psych Sci](https://swh.princeton.edu/~sjleslie/Subtle%20Linguistic%20Cues.pdf)
- **Parents can be trained in a simple prompt script, and they keep using it.** Dialogic reading teaches adults:
  - **PEER**: Prompt, Evaluate, Expand, Repeat.
  - **CROWD**: Completion, Recall, Open-ended, Wh- and Distancing prompts.

  It helps receptive and expressive language under age 5, and trained parents were still using it two years later. **Caveats:** in 46 studies, only 2 were RCTs, samples were small and the authors call the effect "modest". *Moderate.* [Pillinger & Vardy 2022 systematic review (Reach Out and Read)](https://reachoutandread.org/pillinger_2022_a-story-so-far-a-systematic-review-of-the-dialogic-reading-literature)
- **Small, regular nudges change parent behaviour.** READY4K!, an 8-month text-message programme for parents of preschoolers, raised parental involvement at home and school by 0.15–0.29 SD and early literacy by about 0.11 SD. *Moderate–strong [causal, RCT].* [York, Loeb & Doss 2018, J Human Resources (NBER WP)](https://www.nber.org/papers/W20659); [Annenberg summary](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/one-step-time-effects-early-literacy-text-messaging-program-parents-preschoolers)
- **Active parent participation is a common ingredient of the classes that showed effects** (Gerry et al.; Music Together). In Gerry et al., parents also journaled and repeated activities daily at home with a CD ([Gerry et al. 2012](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/GerryUnrauTrainor_2012.pdf)). *Moderate.*
- **Gap:** we found no study that coaches *non-musician* parents specifically for music co-teaching. Everything above is borrowed from praise, reading and nudge research.

## 5. Screen-time guidance relevant to ages 2–5

- **AAP 2016 ("Media and Young Minds"):**
  - Under 18 months: avoid screens except video chat.
  - 18–24 months: if screens are introduced, choose high-quality content and co-view.
  - Ages 2–5: up to **1 hour a day of high-quality programming, co-viewed**.
  - The key factor in learning is parents "watching with them and reteaching the content". The statement recommends "shared use between parent and child".
  - Toddlers "have difficulty transferring that knowledge to their 3-dimensional experience" (the transfer deficit).
  - Most "educational" apps have no evidence of efficacy.
  - Avoid "apps with lots of distracting content".
  - Industry should "formally and scientifically evaluate before making educational claims".

  *Strong (official guidance).* [ScienceDaily summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/10/161021121843.htm); [full statement copy (NHS Norfolk host)](https://www.justonenorfolk.nhs.uk/media/i5cn5lgj/docobookcom_media-and-young-minds.pdf)
- **AAP 2026 ("Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents", Pediatrics, Feb 2026) replaces the 2016 guidance.**
  - It moves "beyond simple screen-time limits" to "quality, context and conversation". The releases we read set no numeric limit.
  - Families: "Watch and play alongside your kids… the best way to know what they're consuming". Shared family devices encourage co-use.
  - It flags "autoplay, endless scrolling and targeted ads" and "rewards that keep them playing".
  - Industry should switch autoplay off by default, minimise features that prolong engagement, add nudges to disengage, run no targeted ads to minors and make privacy the default.

  **Caveat:** we read the AAP release and the HealthyChildren explainer, not the full statement. Check it for any surviving age-specific limits. *Strong (official guidance).* [AAP news release, 20 Jan 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2026/american-academy-of-pediatrics-calls-for-creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world/); [HealthyChildren explainer](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Helping-Kids-Thrive-in-a-Digital-World-AAP-Policy-Explained.aspx); [Deseret News](https://www.deseret.com/family/2026/01/21/limiting-screen-time-not-enough-protect-children-social-media); [EdSurge](https://edsurge.com/news/2026-02-05-new-aap-screen-time-recommendations-focus-less-on-screens-more-on-family-time). EdSurge says the 2016 limit was "two hours", which is wrong for ages 2–5; it was one hour.
- **WHO 2019:** for ages 2–4, sedentary screen time should be "no more than 1 hour; less is better", with none at age 1. The WHO prefers "quality sedentary time spent in interactive non-screen-based activities with a caregiver", naming books, storytelling, **songs** and puzzles. Ages 3–4 also need 180 minutes of activity a day. *Strong (official guideline).* [WHO, 24 Apr 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more)
- **What "high quality" means in practice: the four pillars of learning.** Learning is more likely when an app is:
  - **active** ("minds-on", not just tapping);
  - **engaged**, with no distracting side games or noise;
  - **meaningful**, connected to the child's life;
  - **socially interactive**, discussed with parents.

  Scaffolded exploration toward a clear learning goal also helps. *Moderate (expert review of the learning sciences).* [Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015, PSPI (APS)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html)
- **Touchscreen learning beats some alternatives, but not real objects or paper.** A meta-analysis of 36 studies (k = 79, n = 4,206, ages 0–5) found d = 0.46 overall, with very high heterogeneity. Against physical objects the effect was d = 0.10 and against paper d = 0.11, both not significant. Benefits grew with age, and caregiver interaction was not examined. *Moderate.* [Xie et al. 2018, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02580/full)

## 6. Attention span and session length, ages 2–6

- **By age 5, children "pay attention for 5 to 10 minutes during activities".** The CDC gives story time and crafts as examples and says explicitly that screen time does not count. *Strong as a population norm.* [CDC milestones (NJ DoE adaptation)](https://www.nj.gov/education/specialed/monitor/docs/CDCMilestones.pdf)
- **Sustained attention in free play grows until about age 4 and then plateaus.** Older children are more likely to return to an activity after a break (n = 95, aged 2–6). *Weak–moderate [correlational, cross-sectional].* [Sarid & Breznitz 1997, IJBD](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/developmental-aspects-of-sustained-attention-among-2-to-6-year-ol/)
- **Ten-minute daily sessions built from two 5-minute tasks produced learning at ages 5–6** ([Degé & Schwarzer 2011](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00124/full)). In the joint-music prosocial experiment, a 3-minute music phase was enough to shift behaviour ([Kirschner & Tomasello 2010](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/Elsevier/Kirschner_Joint_EvolHumBeh_2010_1552719.pdf)). *Weak–moderate.*
- **Gap:** we found no study comparing music-lesson lengths for ages 2–4. "Two to three minutes per year of age" is folk wisdom; we could not find a primary source for it.

## 7. Adaptive or intelligent tutoring, and rhythm training

### Adaptive tutoring for young children
- **Personalisation by a social robot improved vocabulary learning at ages 4–6.** n = 67 English-language learners over three months in schools. A robot whose story choices adapted to each child's affective cues beat a fixed-curriculum robot and no robot on word learning, retention and target syntax. *Weak–moderate [causal] (one study; abstract only).* [Park et al. 2019, AAAI (MIT DSpace)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137140)
- **Interaction helps only when it is specific to the content, and the effect depends on age.**
  - 116 toddlers aged 24–36 months were randomised to watch a word-teaching video passively, touch anywhere to advance it, or touch the specific target.
  - "Touch anywhere" did not help.
  - "Touch the target" helped the youngest toddlers (about 24–27 months) but *hurt* those aged about 28–32 months.

  *Moderate [causal].* [Kirkorian, Choi & Pempek 2016, Child Dev](https://core.ac.uk/download/270042326.pdf)
- **Gap:** we found no study of adaptive or intelligent music tutors for ages 2–6. Evidence for an "adaptive teacher" is indirect: the four pillars, contingency, live-partner advantages and the robot personalisation study.

### Rhythm and beat synchronisation, language and reading
- **Preschoolers who can drum in time also do better on pre-reading skills.** Children who synchronised outperformed those who did not on:
  - phonological awareness (d = 1.33, 4-year-olds only);
  - auditory short-term memory (d = 0.74);
  - rapid naming (d = 0.52–0.77).

  n = 35, replicated in n = 156 aged 3–5. **This is correlational.** The authors call for longitudinal work and did not test whether training helps. *Moderate [correlational].* [Woodruff Carr et al. 2014](https://brainvolts.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/boxtrx/PNAS-2014-Woodruff%20Carr-1406219111.pdf); [Bonacina et al. 2021](https://brainvolts.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Bonacina_et_al-2021-npj_Science_of_Learning.pdf)
- **Music training has a small effect on phonological awareness and none on reading fluency.** A meta-analysis of 13 studies (n = 901) found d ≈ 0.2 for phonological awareness and no reliable effect on reading fluency. Rhyming effects grew with training hours. *Moderate.* [Gordon, Fehd & McCandliss 2015 (VUMC abstract)](https://www.vumc.org/music-cognition-lab/publication/does-music-training-enhance-literacy-skills-meta-analysis)
- **Music training may yield small auditory and linguistic gains**, with possible publication bias. 62 longitudinal studies, n = 3,928. *Moderate.* [Neves et al. 2022 (ISCTE repository)](https://repositorio-iul.iscte.pt/handle/10071/26564?mode=full)
- **Music training does not improve general cognition or academic achievement.** g ≈ 0 across 6,984 children. Only low-quality designs (non-random, passive controls) showed g ≈ 0.2. *Strong (null for far transfer).* [Sala & Gobet 2020, Memory & Cognition (LSE)](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/104996)

**Bottom line for section 7.** Beat-keeping is a **marker** that correlates with pre-reading skills. Training gives at most **small** gains in phonological awareness and **no** reliable general cognitive benefit.

## 8. Family and social play (grandparents, family concerts)

- **Live, contingent video chat counts as real interaction for toddlers; recorded video does not.** In Myers et al. 2016 (n = 60, aged 1–2), learning and synchronised responses such as imitative clapping happened only in the live condition. The AAP exempts video chat even for under-18-month-olds ([AAP 2016](https://www.justonenorfolk.nhs.uk/media/i5cn5lgj/docobookcom_media-and-young-minds.pdf)). *Moderate [causal] (press release).* [ISPR summary](https://ispr.info/2016/07/19/new-study-of-toddlers-sheds-light-on-value-of-facetime-video-chat-as-meaningful-interaction/)
- **Intergenerational music making has positive wellbeing reports for both children and older adults, but the evidence is thin.** This comes from a review chapter and a care-home pilot of preschoolers with older adults over six weekly sessions. Neither reports sample sizes or statistics. *Weak.* [Varvarigou et al. 2015, Oxford Textbook chapter](https://pure.canterbury.ac.uk/en/publications/intergenerational-music-making-a-vehicle-for-active-ageing-for-ch-2/); [Plymouth, Making Bridges with Music](https://www.plymouth.ac.uk/research/education/inclusion/evaluation-of-the-making-bridges-with-music-intervention)
- **Making music in sync increases short-term helping and cooperation** in infants and 4-year-olds ([Cirelli 2014](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/CirelliEtAl2014); [Kirschner & Tomasello 2010](https://www.eva.mpg.de/documents/Elsevier/Kirschner_Joint_EvolHumBeh_2010_1552719.pdf)). This supports *playing together* as the unit of social play, more than performing *for* someone. *Moderate [causal], short-term.*
- **Gap:** no study of "family concerts" or recorded performances for grandparents as a learning or bonding mechanism.

## Implications for Little Duet (ranked)

1. **Make parent co-play the core loop and build the Parent Coaching bet first.** This is where the evidence is strongest. Active participation with a parent beats passive exposure, co-use is the central AAP recommendation in 2016 and 2026, and nudges change parent behaviour. Give the parent one concrete action per class segment ("clap with her on the beat", "echo his note back"), delivered in a format they can read at a glance. Sources: [Gerry 2012](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/GerryUnrauTrainor_2012.pdf), [AAP 2016](https://www.justonenorfolk.nhs.uk/media/i5cn5lgj/docobookcom_media-and-young-minds.pdf), [AAP 2026](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Helping-Kids-Thrive-in-a-Digital-World-AAP-Policy-Explained.aspx), [READY4K](https://www.nber.org/papers/W20659).
2. **Write a praise style guide for both the teacher voice and the parent prompts.**
   - Praise the process and the action: "You kept trying that jump!" or "You listened and found it."
   - Never use trait labels: "You're so talented" or "a little pianist".
   - Use "let's play" framing, not identity framing.
   - Make this a lint rule next to the existing smarter/brain/IQ blocker.
   Sources: [Gunderson](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5826820), [Cimpian](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children), [Rhodes 2019](https://swh.princeton.edu/~sjleslie/Subtle%20Linguistic%20Cues.pdf).
3. **The adaptive teacher should adapt *to the child's tempo* and never penalise being off the beat.**
   - Under 4, keep songs in roughly the 100–150 bpm band, nearer 120 bpm for age 3.
   - Let the accompaniment follow the child instead of a fixed click.
   - Slow songs are harder than fast ones for young children.
   - Treat "played the right key at roughly the right time" as success. Even 24–37% of children aged 3–5 cannot sync with an adult.
   Sources: [Provasi 2014](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01048/full), [Bonacina 2021](https://brainvolts.northwestern.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Bonacina_et_al-2021-npj_Science_of_Learning.pdf).
4. **Band content by age and skill, not by song catalogue.**
   - Ages 2–3: movement, beat and echo games. Whole-hand or single big-zone presses. The parent plays the melody while the child adds one note or the beat.
   - Age 4: single-finger playing and short 2–4-note patterns.
   - Ages 5–6: finger numbers, as an option.
   Mark finger work as unvalidated, since there is no direct evidence under 5. Sources: [CDC](https://www.nj.gov/education/specialed/monitor/docs/CDCMilestones.pdf), [Roeder 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2564283).
5. **Build "Beyond the piano" as rhythm, movement and singing modules that a parent and child do together, not solo.**
   - A live partner improves synchronisation even at 2.5.
   - Singing and movement first is the consensus of every early-childhood method.
   - WHO names songs as preferred quality time.
   - Joint drumming is the exact manipulation in the prosocial studies.
   Sources: [Kirschner 2009](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458233), [NAfME](https://nafme.org/blog/kodaly-orff-and-dalcroze-a-whos-who-and-whats-what/), [WHO](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more).
6. **Design singing features to fit the child's voice.**
   - Sing-along keys in about D4–A4.
   - Echo phrases (call and response) rather than whole songs.
   - Offer a neutral-syllable ("la") mode.
   - Do not score pitch accuracy for children under 5; at most, reward attempts.
   Sources: [Welch 2009](https://www.imerc.org/papers/nsp/welch2009.pdf), [Pereira 2019](https://core.ac.uk/download/322889247.pdf).
7. **Keep classes at 3–5 minutes and favour repetition and spacing over novelty.**
   - Replay the same song across days, like Suzuki review pieces.
   - Spread learning across short sessions.
   - Do not auto-advance to the next class.
   Sources: [Horst 2011](https://doaj.org/article/0b7e5d967574458bacca6716c88db682), [Vlach 2008](https://babytalk.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/271/2021/03/Vlach_Sandhofer_Kornell_2008.pdf), [CDC 5–10 min](https://www.nj.gov/education/specialed/monitor/docs/CDCMilestones.pdf), [AAP 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2026/american-academy-of-pediatrics-calls-for-creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world/).
8. **Make real-piano mode and an off-screen "take it away" activity the default.** End each class with an unplugged prompt, such as singing it in the bath or clapping it on the walk. This fights the transfer deficit, fits WHO's "interactive non-screen" time and keeps screen minutes low. Sources: [AAP 2016](https://www.justonenorfolk.nhs.uk/media/i5cn5lgj/docobookcom_media-and-young-minds.pdf), [Xie 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02580/full).
9. **Build in engagement guardrails from the start.**
   - No autoplay.
   - No variable-reward loops or rewards "that keep them playing".
   - No ads.
   - A visible "we're done for today" stop cue.
   This is now explicit AAP guidance for industry and a trust signal for parents. Source: [AAP 2026](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2026/american-academy-of-pediatrics-calls-for-creating-a-child-friendly-digital-world/).
10. **Put family and social play on live duets over video call, with grandparent and child playing together, ahead of recorded "family concerts".** Live contingent video works for toddlers and recorded video does not. Treat recordings as celebration and keepsakes, not as the learning mechanism. This bet has the thinnest evidence of the four. Sources: [Myers 2016](https://ispr.info/2016/07/19/new-study-of-toddlers-sheds-light-on-value-of-facetime-video-chat-as-meaningful-interaction/), [Varvarigou 2015](https://pure.canterbury.ac.uk/en/publications/intergenerational-music-making-a-vehicle-for-active-ageing-for-ch-2/).
11. **Make adaptive feedback specific to the content.** For example, highlight *the* key to press, not "tap anywhere". Expect the effects to vary by age, and A/B test by age band. Source: [Kirkorian 2016](https://core.ac.uk/download/270042326.pdf).
12. **Run our own evaluation before making any learning claim.** Measures could be parent–child turn-taking during class, a steady-beat task (march-and-clap is reliable, ICC .96) and parent-reported co-play frequency. The AAP asks industry to evaluate before making educational claims. Sources: [AAP 2016](https://www.justonenorfolk.nhs.uk/media/i5cn5lgj/docobookcom_media-and-young-minds.pdf), [Tuominen 2020](https://ukkinstituutti.fi/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/2059204320962281.pdf).

## Claims we may and may not make

### We may say (skills wording; each claim is accurate on its face)
- "Designed for a parent and child to play *together*." This matches the AAP and WHO emphasis on co-use and caregiver interaction. Do not imply endorsement.
- "Practise **steady beat, listening, echoing, singing, taking turns and finding keys**." These name the skills practised and promise no outcome.
- "Short 3–5 minute classes sized for young children."
- "Inspired by long-standing early-childhood music approaches: singing, movement, repetition, call and response, parent as partner."
- "Most families already sing together. Little Duet gives that time a little structure." Basis: 69% of parents sing daily ([Yan 2021](https://www.harvardlds.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/2021_YanEtAl_PTRSB.pdf)).
- "Works on a real piano, or anywhere with the on-screen keys, with ideas to keep the music going off-screen."
- "No ads. No autoplay." Say this only if true.

### We must not say
- Anything about the **brain, IQ, intelligence, "smarter" or "neural" development**. This is a product rule, and the best meta-analysis finds no cognitive or academic benefit ([Sala & Gobet 2020](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/104996); [Mehr 2013](https://doaj.org/article/06f7cedd58de4b2988c4a8d83c05cc3d)).
- "Boosts reading, language, vocabulary or school readiness." The rhythm–reading link is correlational, training effects are small (d ≈ 0.2 for phonological awareness) and there is none for fluency ([Gordon 2015](https://www.vumc.org/music-cognition-lab/publication/does-music-training-enhance-literacy-skills-meta-analysis)). The LSAC vocabulary link vanished after controlling for reading ([Williams 2015](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1496686/1/Welch_final.pdf)).
- "Improves self-regulation, attention or emotional regulation." The evidence is correlational or quasi-experimental ([Williams 2015](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1496686/1/Welch_final.pdf); [Winsler 2011](https://www.artsedsearch.org/?p=1707)).
- "Makes kids kinder or more cooperative," or that it "builds bonding" as an outcome. The prosocial effects are short-term lab effects with peers or experimenters, not parents, and not with our product. "Time together" phrasing is fine; outcome claims are not.
- "Scientifically proven", "clinically proven", "research-backed results" or "educational" as an outcome claim, until Little Duet itself has been evaluated (AAP 2016). We may cite research *informing the design* in a separate, carefully worded science page.
- "AAP- or WHO-approved/recommended app," or any suggestion that the app fits inside an official screen-time allowance.
- "Teaches 2-year-olds to play piano" or "finger independence at 3". This is not supported, and toddlers' singing and beat are not yet accurate.
- "AI teacher replaces lessons or teachers." See the regulatory scrutiny noted in [market.md](market.md).
- Any "Mozart effect", "musical genius" or "talent" framing. This conflicts with the process-praise guide as well as the evidence.

## Open gaps worth commissioning or testing ourselves
1. Piano and keyboard motor readiness at ages 2–5 (no primary data found).
2. Session-length experiments for music at ages 2–4.
3. Coaching non-musician parents specifically for music; we have only analogies from reading and praise research.
4. Adaptive or AI music tutors for preschoolers (no studies found).
5. Whether app-guided parent–child music produces the interaction gains seen in live classes such as Music Together, which is the core assumption behind Little Duet.
6. Intergenerational or remote music play outcomes with real measures.
